Machine Learning for Gamma-Ray Bursts

Machine Learning, Statistical Modelling and Uncertainty-Aware Reconstruction of High-Energy Time Series

Research Overview

My machine-learning research focuses on reconstructing and analysing noisy, irregularly sampled time-series data from gamma-ray bursts (GRBs). I developed machine-learning and statistical methods to recover the underlying temporal behaviour of GRB X-ray light curves and quantify the uncertainty associated with the reconstruction.

The project combines data preprocessing, probabilistic modelling, deep learning, model validation and uncertainty estimation. The same research workflow has shaped how I approach difficult data-driven problems: define the problem carefully, build a model, test it against controlled validation data, quantify uncertainty, and examine where and why the model fails.

My published work on GRB light-curve reconstruction demonstrates an end-to-end data science workflow: handling irregular data, constructing probabilistic models, comparing reconstruction methods, validating predictions and reporting uncertainty rather than relying only on point estimates.

ReFANN: Light-Curve Reconstruction

I worked on ReFANN, a machine-learning framework for reconstructing GRB X-ray light curves. The problem is challenging because the observations can contain gaps, irregular sampling, measurement uncertainty and complex temporal behaviour.

I investigated neural-network and probabilistic approaches for recovering the underlying light curve while retaining information about the uncertainty of the reconstruction.

Deep Gaussian Processes

A central component of the work involved Gaussian-process based modelling, including Deep Gaussian Processes. These models provide a flexible way to represent non-linear temporal structure while retaining a probabilistic description of the prediction.

This gave me practical experience working with models where uncertainty is part of the output rather than something added after the prediction.

Model Validation

I used cross-validation and bootstrap resampling to test the robustness of the reconstruction methods. Rather than evaluating a model on a single split, I examined how its performance changed across different samples and perturbations of the data.

I also analysed reconstruction errors and their distributions to understand systematic differences between the reconstructed and observed light curves.

Time-Series and Statistical Modelling

Working with GRB light curves required careful treatment of temporal structure, missing observations and measurement noise. I used statistical modelling and probabilistic methods to separate the inferred underlying signal from observational uncertainty.

This work developed my interest in quantitative research where noisy observations must be converted into robust statistical conclusions.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks

In a separate machine-learning project, I implemented Physics-Informed Neural Networks in PyTorch for differential equations, including 1D, 2D and 3D Burgers' equations and the 1D Euler equation for the Sod shock-tube problem.

I compared the neural-network solutions with classical numerical approaches and analytical solutions, and investigated how the choice of training and numerical parameters affected stability and accuracy.

Research Workflow

Across these projects, I have worked through the complete computational research cycle:

  • Cleaning and structuring real-world datasets
  • Designing statistical and machine-learning models
  • Training models and tuning model parameters
  • Cross-validation and bootstrap-based robustness checks
  • Quantifying prediction uncertainty
  • Comparing models using quantitative error measures
  • Investigating failure modes and model limitations
  • Reproducing and validating results computationally

Quantitative Research Perspective

The methods I use are directly relevant to quantitative, data-driven research: large datasets, noisy observations, statistical inference, time-series modelling, probabilistic prediction, model validation and reproducible computational experiments.

I am particularly interested in problems where the useful signal is small compared with the noise and where model performance has to be tested rigorously rather than assumed from a single result. This is also why I place strong emphasis on out-of-sample validation, uncertainty estimation and understanding model failure modes.

I have not used this work to claim financial-market performance. Instead, it demonstrates the research and modelling skills I bring to quantitative problems: working with complex data, building statistical models, testing hypotheses, validating results and turning computational experiments into reproducible conclusions.
