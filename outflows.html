Ultra Fast Outflows

Searching for High-Velocity Outflows in Active Galactic Nuclei

Research Overview

Ultra-fast outflows (UFOs) are highly ionised winds launched from the innermost regions of active galactic nuclei, with velocities reaching a substantial fraction of the speed of light. These outflows provide a direct probe of the extreme environment close to a supermassive black hole and may carry significant amounts of energy and momentum into the host galaxy.

My work focuses on systematically searching for these high-velocity outflows using archival XMM-Newton observations. I developed a Python-based analysis pipeline to process large astronomical datasets, select high-quality observations and identify statistically significant absorption features associated with ionised outflowing gas.

Core idea: convert a large astronomical archive into a systematic statistical search problem — automatically identify promising datasets, detect candidate spectral features, test their significance, and then use physical photoionisation models to characterise the underlying gas.

Why Ultra-Fast Outflows Matter

Because these winds originate close to the central black hole, their velocity, ionisation state and column density provide information about the conditions of the innermost accretion environment. Measuring their properties can therefore help constrain how accretion energy is transferred from the central engine into the surrounding environment.

Understanding these outflows is also important for studying AGN feedback: energetic winds can transport momentum and energy into the host galaxy and potentially influence the evolution of gas, star formation and the growth of the central black hole.

Large-Scale Data Analysis

I built a Python pipeline to cross-match large AGN catalogues with the XMM-Newton archive and systematically identify observations suitable for high-quality UFO searches.

Searching for Outflows

The search focuses on absorption features in the X-ray spectrum produced by highly ionised gas moving at relativistic velocities. In particular, features associated with highly ionised iron in the Fe-K energy band can provide a powerful signature of fast outflowing material.

I first establish a physically motivated continuum model and then search for statistically significant deviations from that baseline. Candidate absorption features are subsequently tested using alternative spectral models and statistical simulations.

Photoionisation Modelling

Detecting an absorption feature is only the first step. To determine the physical properties of the outflowing gas, I use photoionisation modelling to connect the observed absorption spectrum with parameters such as ionisation, column density and turbulent velocity.

I developed custom XSTAR photoionisation grids for both soft-X-ray absorption features and Fe-K absorption signatures when the available models were insufficient to describe the observed spectra.

These grids allow the observed absorption to be interpreted in terms of the physical state of the gas and provide a consistent framework for comparing different outflow candidates.

Statistical Inference

A central part of the analysis is determining whether an apparent spectral feature represents a genuine physical signal or a statistical fluctuation. I use spectral fitting, parameter estimation, uncertainty analysis and Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate candidate features.

For more complex models, I use Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods to explore posterior parameter distributions, correlations and parameter degeneracies.

Quantitative Analysis Workflow

The project follows a reproducible analysis pipeline in which each stage reduces the search space while preserving statistically meaningful information.

01 — Data Selection

Large catalogues and archival observations are filtered using objective quality criteria to construct a statistically useful analysis sample.

02 — Signal Detection

Automated spectral analysis is used to identify candidate absorption features across a large number of observations.

03 — Model Comparison

Alternative spectral models are compared to determine whether additional physical components are statistically supported by the data.

04 — Parameter Inference

Physical parameters and their uncertainties are estimated using spectral fitting, simulations and MCMC-based inference.

Key Results

The search identified sources showing both warm absorption and ultra-fast outflows through absorption features associated with highly ionised gas. Several of the identified sources provide new candidates for studying fast winds across different ionisation phases.

In one luminous quasar, the analysis revealed evidence for multi-phase ultra-fast outflows with inferred velocities of approximately 0.22c and 0.32c.

An important aspect of the project is the identification of outflows across different energy regimes. Soft-X-ray absorption can trace lower ionisation phases of the wind, while Fe-K absorption provides a probe of highly ionised material closer to the central engine.

Computational Approach

Although the scientific application is astrophysics, the underlying workflow is fundamentally quantitative: large-scale data processing, feature detection, statistical hypothesis testing, model comparison, parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification.

The project has strengthened my ability to turn complex, noisy datasets into reproducible quantitative analyses while carefully distinguishing statistically significant signals from fluctuations and modelling uncertainties.
