Machine Learning for Gamma-Ray Bursts

Probabilistic modelling, deep learning and uncertainty-aware reconstruction of irregular astronomical time-series data.

Overview

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are among the most energetic transient phenomena in the Universe. Their X-ray light curves contain information about the evolution of the relativistic outflow and the physical processes operating after the initial burst.

A major challenge is that astronomical observations are often noisy, irregularly sampled and affected by observational gaps. My work focuses on reconstructing these missing regions while quantifying the uncertainty associated with the predictions.

Working with the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, I developed and evaluated multiple machine-learning approaches for GRB X-ray light-curve reconstruction, including ReFANN, Siamese Neural Networks and Deep Gaussian Processes.

The Problem

Observational gaps can make it difficult to accurately determine important properties of GRB light curves. Missing data can propagate into uncertainties in quantities such as the plateau end time, flux and temporal slope.

Rather than treating reconstruction as simple interpolation, I approached it as a probabilistic time-series inference problem: infer the underlying temporal behaviour from incomplete and noisy observations while retaining information about uncertainty.

Machine-Learning Framework

01 — ReFANN

Uncertainty-Aware Neural Network

I implemented ReFANN using heteroscedastic prediction heads to reconstruct missing portions of GRB light curves while estimating predictive uncertainty.

02 — Deep Gaussian Processes

Probabilistic Time-Series Modelling

Deep Gaussian Processes were used to model complex temporal structure while naturally representing uncertainty in the reconstructed light curve.

03 — Siamese Networks

Learning Temporal Similarity

I explored Siamese architectures that compare a partially observed light curve with reference information to learn useful representations for reconstruction.

04 — Physics-Informed Learning

Constrained Model Training

I incorporated physics-informed constraints into the learning objective alongside probabilistic loss functions to encourage physically meaningful reconstructions.

Statistical Validation

Model performance was evaluated using cross-validation, bootstrap resampling and uncertainty estimation rather than relying on a single train-test split.

Cross-Validation

Five-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate whether reconstruction performance generalised beyond the training subset.

Bootstrap Resampling

Bootstrap resampling was used to assess the robustness of model-derived statistics and reconstruction performance.

Uncertainty Quantification

Predictive uncertainty was explicitly evaluated rather than reporting reconstructed values without an estimate of their reliability.

Error Analysis

Reconstruction quality was assessed using quantitative error metrics and comparison against benchmark approaches.

Results

522 / 545 GRBs processed through the reconstruction pipeline.

62% mean parameter uncertainty reduction compared with the benchmark reported in the project.

0.0196 test mean-squared error achieved by the ReFANN pipeline.

The resulting reconstructions improve the reliability of physically meaningful GRB light-curve parameters and can strengthen their use in statistical and cosmological studies.

Why This Work Matters Beyond Astrophysics

Although the application is astrophysical, the underlying problem is fundamentally quantitative: extracting signal from noisy, incomplete and irregularly sampled time-series data.

The same computational principles appear in quantitative research, where models must identify structure in noisy data, quantify uncertainty and be validated against unseen samples.

Computational Work

The project involved designing the workflow from data preparation through model training, evaluation and statistical analysis.

I worked extensively with Python and PyTorch, implementing neural architectures, probabilistic models, uncertainty estimation and automated data-processing workflows.

This required careful preprocessing, numerical optimisation, reproducible experiments, model comparison and analysis of failure cases rather than treating the machine-learning model as a black box.
