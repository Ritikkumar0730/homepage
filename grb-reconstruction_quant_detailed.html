Reconstructing Missing Information in GRB X-ray Light Curves

My work with Prof. Maria G. Dainotti focuses on a practical problem: how can we recover missing parts of a noisy and irregular time series without losing the information contained in the data or making the model more confident than it should be?

Current paper on arXiv Published JHEAp paper Back to research

What is the problem?

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are observed over time using instruments such as Swift-XRT. The resulting X-ray light curves are not always complete. There can be gaps because of observational constraints, and these gaps can occur exactly where an important feature of the light curve is changing.

The problem is therefore not simply to draw a smooth line between two observations. We want to reconstruct the missing part in a way that follows the behaviour of the observed data, gives a useful estimate of uncertainty, and allows the important light-curve parameters to be measured more reliably.

Paper 1 — Multi-model framework

Journal of High Energy Astrophysics · 2026

Multi-model framework for reconstructing gamma-ray burst light curves

A. Kaushal, A. Manchanda, M. G. Dainotti, Ritik Kumar et al.

This paper compares several different ways of reconstructing missing parts of GRB X-ray light curves. Instead of assuming that one model must be best, we put different statistical and machine-learning approaches into a common framework and compare how well they recover the important light-curve parameters.

Data

The study uses a sample of 521 GRBs from the Swift BAT-XRT repository, with light curves containing observational gaps.

Models compared

Deep Gaussian Processes, Temporal Convolutional Networks, CNN-BiLSTM, Bayesian Neural Networks, polynomial fitting, isotonic regression, and quartic smoothing splines.

What this work taught me

The main lesson was that a model should not be judged only by how closely it follows the observed data. A useful model also needs to give sensible uncertainty estimates and behave reliably when the data are sparse or noisy. Comparing several models under the same evaluation setup made the trade-offs much clearer.

Paper 2 — Physics-informed and data-driven reconstruction

arXiv · 2026 · submitted for peer review

Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Data-Driven Models for GRB X-ray Light-Curve Gap Reconstruction

A. Garg, Ritik Kumar, M. G. Dainotti et al.

This work takes the reconstruction problem further. The main question was whether adding useful prior information can improve reconstruction without making the model too rigid. We compare a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) with three data-driven approaches: ReFANN, a modified Siamese network, and Polynomial Quantile Regression.

What makes this difficult?

The GRB data are noisy, irregularly sampled and can contain large gaps. Different bursts can also have very different shapes, so a single fixed functional form does not work equally well for all of them.

What we compare

Four models are tested using the same overall reconstruction framework, allowing their uncertainty reduction, robustness and outlier behaviour to be compared directly.

What I actually worked on

My role in these projects was not just to run an existing model and report the result. I worked on the parts that make a model useful in practice: building the models, training them, checking the uncertainty, tuning the settings, testing different choices, and finding out what went wrong when training became unstable. Working with hundreds of noisy and incomplete time series also meant that I had to think carefully about whether a result was actually reliable or just looked good on one dataset.

1. Building the probabilistic model

For the first paper, I worked on the Deep Gaussian Process part of the study. I implemented a two-layer variational Deep GP using GPyTorch and used it to reconstruct missing parts of the GRB light curves. I was interested not only in the reconstructed curve, but also in the uncertainty around that prediction.

  • Built and trained the Deep GP model in Python/GPyTorch.
  • Worked with irregular and noisy time-series data.
  • Compared its reconstruction and uncertainty with other models.

2. Building uncertainty-aware neural networks

In the second paper, I implemented the uncertainty-aware neural network approaches in PyTorch. The goal was to make the model give both a prediction and a measure of how uncertain that prediction was, rather than giving one number and treating it as exact.

  • Implemented neural-network training and prediction pipelines.
  • Used a Gaussian negative log-likelihood to model prediction uncertainty.
  • Checked the balance between reconstruction quality and uncertainty.

3. Siamese network and reference information

I also worked on the modified Siamese network used in the second paper. The idea was to use a reference light curve together with the incomplete target curve. I implemented the reference-anchor fusion and tested whether this extra information could make the reconstruction more stable.

  • Implemented the Siamese architecture and reference-target fusion.
  • Tested the model on incomplete observations.
  • Measured changes in parameter uncertainty and outlier behaviour.

4. Quantile regression

I implemented polynomial quantile regression as another way to describe uncertainty. Instead of predicting only the average behaviour, the model estimates different parts of the distribution. I used the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles with pinball loss and compared the resulting intervals with the neural-network models.

  • Implemented multiple quantiles: 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9.
  • Used pinball loss for training.
  • Compared uncertainty intervals and outlier fractions.

5. Finding and fixing a training failure

One of the most useful parts of the project was debugging a PINN that was not learning the way we expected. The model could reduce the physics loss but move towards an almost constant solution. Instead of treating this as just a bad result, I tested the training process and changed the way the losses were balanced.

  • Used a value-space loss to better control the reconstruction.
  • Introduced a gradual warm-up of the physics-loss weight.
  • Used EMA-based loss normalisation to keep the loss terms balanced.
  • Added an automatic restart when a training run failed.

6. Hyperparameter search and validation

I did not want the final comparison to depend on one lucky set of hyperparameters. I used Optuna to search the model settings and used 5-fold cross-validation to check how well the choices held up across different subsets of the data.

  • Systematic hyperparameter optimisation with Optuna.
  • 5-fold cross-validation for more reliable evaluation.
  • Ablation tests to see which modelling choices actually mattered.

7. Comparing models fairly

Across the two papers, I worked with several very different approaches: Gaussian Processes, neural networks, Bayesian models, Siamese networks, PINNs, polynomial models, quantile regression and smoothing methods. A big part of my work was making the comparison meaningful rather than looking at one metric and declaring a winner.

  • Compared prediction quality, uncertainty and outlier behaviour.
  • Looked for trade-offs instead of relying on a single score.
  • Checked whether improvements were robust across the dataset.

8. Working with messy real data

The data were not clean textbook examples. The light curves can be noisy, irregularly sampled, incomplete and very different from one another. This forced me to think about what the model can really learn from the available information and when a prediction should be treated with caution.

  • Worked with hundreds of independent GRB light curves.
  • Handled missing regions and irregular sampling.
  • Used common evaluation procedures to keep comparisons consistent.

Why these methods are useful beyond astrophysics

The problem is astrophysics, but the way I solved it is much broader.

I started with GRB light curves because that is the research problem I work on. But the difficult part of the problem is familiar in many quantitative settings: the data are noisy, some observations are missing, the sampling is not perfectly regular, and the model can be uncertain or unstable.

Because of this, I learned to think about more than just getting a model to fit the data. I ask whether the model is making a sensible prediction, how sensitive the result is to the choices I made, how uncertainty should be measured, and what happens when the model starts to fail. I also learned that the best model is not always the one with the best single score; reliability and robustness matter too.

That way of working is what I want to carry into other quantitative problems: take difficult data, build a model, test it properly, find its weak points, and understand how much confidence I should place in the result.

A short summary

Across these two papers, I worked on the full modelling process rather than only one part of it. I built and tested probabilistic and neural models, worked on uncertainty estimates, tuned and validated the models, debugged unstable training, and compared different approaches on noisy and incomplete data. The main thing I took from this work is a practical habit: do not stop when a model gives a good-looking answer. Check why it works, how stable it is, how uncertain it is, and when it can fail.
